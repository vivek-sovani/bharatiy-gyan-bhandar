📖 *दिवस २६ · Day 26*

*ऋतुचर्या*
_वर्षाचे सहा ऋतू, आणि प्रत्येकासाठी वेगळा आहार._

आपण वर्षाचे चार ऋतू मानतो. भारतीय परंपरा सहा मानते. वसंत, ग्रीष्म, वर्षा, शरद, हेमंत आणि शिशिर. प्रत्येक ऋतू साधारण दोन महिन्यांचा.

आयुर्वेदाचा विचार असा की ऋतू बदलला की शरीरही बदलतं. म्हणून आहार-विहारही बदलावा. उन्हाळ्यात थंड, हलकं आणि गोड फळांचं सेवन. पावसात पचायला हलकं, गरम आणि थोडं तूप-मसालेदार. थंडीत ताकद देणारे जड पदार्थ, डिंक-तिळाचे लाडू.

यातला एक छान दाखला पाहा. वसंत ऋतूच्या सुरुवातीला गुढीपाडव्याला कडुनिंबाची पानं गुळासोबत खाण्याची प्रथा आहे. वसंतात कफ वितळतो, म्हणून त्याआधी कडू चव घ्यावी, हा ऋतुचर्येचा नियम. आपण परंपरा म्हणून करतो, पण त्यामागे ऋतूचं गणित आहे.

📖 *Day 26*

*Ṛtucaryā*
_Six seasons, and a different way of eating for each._

We speak of four seasons. The Indian tradition counts six: Vasanta, Grīṣma, Varṣā, Śarad, Hemanta and Śiśira, each lasting about two months.

The Āyurvedic idea is simple. When the season changes, the body changes too, so food and habits should follow. Cool, light, sweet fruit in summer. Warm, easily digested food with a little ghee and spice in the rains. Strength-giving, heavier food in the cold, which is why winter brings sesame and gum laddus.

Here is a lovely example. At the start of spring, on Gudi Padwa, many families eat neem leaves with jaggery. In spring Kapha melts and rises, so the classical rule is to take bitter tastes just before it. We do it as custom, but there is a calculation behind it.

One more rule: at the junction of two seasons, the *ṛtu-sandhi*, change your diet gradually over about fifteen days, not overnight.

_आयुष्याचे चार टप्पे, आणि प्रत्येकाचं स्वतःचं काम._

📖 *Day 27*

*Āśrama*
_Four stages of life, each with its own work._

A Vedic prayer says: "paśyema śaradaḥ śatam", may we see a hundred autumns. A hundred-year life, divided into four parts. That is the idea of *āśrama*.

1️⃣ *Brahmacarya*: the years of learning under a teacher.
2️⃣ *Gārhasthya*: the householder's years of work, family and children.
3️⃣ *Vānaprastha*: handing responsibility to the next generation and stepping back by degrees.
4️⃣ *Sannyāsa*: letting go of everything for the sake of liberation alone.

Behind this lies *ṛṇa-traya*, the three debts one is born owing: to the sages (repaid by study), to the ancestors (repaid by raising the next generation) and to the gods (repaid by ritual and worship). Renouncing before these are settled was seen as escape, not renunciation.

The Mahābhārata calls the householder stage the greatest, since it feeds and supports the other three. Nobody is told they must live all four. The point is that the goal of one age can become an obstacle in another.

*पुरुषार्थ*
_जगण्याची चार ध्येयं, आणि त्यांचा योग्य क्रम._

📖 *Day 28*

*Puruṣārtha*
_Four goals of life, and the right order among them._

Ask a simple question: what should a person aim for in life? The Indian answer may surprise you. It is not renunciation alone. Four things are accepted as worthy goals.

1️⃣ *Dharma*: right conduct, duty.
2️⃣ *Artha*: wealth, security, the means to live.
3️⃣ *Kāma*: pleasure, desire, the enjoyment of art and beauty.
4️⃣ *Mokṣa*: freedom from bondage.

This tradition says openly that earning and enjoying are not wrong. What matters is the order. Dharma is the frame inside which artha and kāma are pursued. Mokṣa is the final end behind all three.

A life of pure kāma without dharma collapses. A life aimed only at mokṣa skips most of what human experience offers.

Scholars note that in earlier texts dharma, artha and kāma were counted together as the *trivarga*, the "three-fold set". Mokṣa came to be set beside them as the fourth. Each goal has its own science: the Arthaśāstra, the Kāmasūtra, the Upaniṣads.

*धर्म*
_सृष्टीच्या शिस्तीचं माणसाच्या वागण्यातलं रूप._

भारताच्या सर्वोच्च न्यायालयाचं ब्रीदवाक्य आहे "यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः", जिथे धर्म तिथे जय. ते महाभारतातून घेतलं आहे. पण *धर्म* म्हणजे नक्की काय?

ऋग्वेदात *ऋत* ही कल्पना आहे. सूर्याचा मार्ग, ऋतूंचं चक्र, नद्यांचं वाहणं, सृष्टीची ही अचूक शिस्त. तिचंच मानवी रूप म्हणजे *सत्यम्*, म्हणजे बोलणं आणि वागणं वास्तवाशी जुळवणं. आणि वागण्याच्या अंगाने तिचंच नाव *धर्म*.

तैत्तिरीय उपनिषदात गुरू निरोप देताना शिष्याला सांगतात: "सत्यं वद, धर्मं चर", खरं बोल, धर्माने वाग.

📖 *Day 29*

*Dharma*
_The order of the universe, as it shows up in how we act._

India's Supreme Court carries the motto "yato dharmas tato jayaḥ": where there is dharma, there is victory. It is taken from the Mahābhārata. But what exactly is *dharma*?

The Ṛgveda speaks of *ṛta*: the sun's circuit, the cycle of seasons, the flow of rivers, the exact discipline of the cosmos. Its human face is *satyam*, bringing word and deed into line with what is real. And its face in conduct is *dharma*.

In the Taittirīya Upaniṣad a teacher's parting charge to the student is "satyaṃ vada, dharmaṃ cara": speak the truth, walk in dharma.

The Mahābhārata says "sūkṣmo dharmasya tattvam": the essence of dharma is subtle, because it shifts with situation and relationship. What it asks for is *vivecana*, the trained ability to read a moment clearly and see what is right. In the Gītā, Arjuna is not given a new rule. He is given clear sight.

And one more line: "dharmo rakṣati rakṣitaḥ", dharma protects those who protect it.

*योग*

📖 *Day 30*

*Yoga*
_The inner practice. A path, not a workout._

Today "yoga" brings postures to mind. But in Patañjali's Yoga-sūtras, āsana is only the third of eight limbs, and it gets just a few lines. One of them says: "sthira-sukham āsanam", the posture should be steady and comfortable.

The word yoga means "to yoke, to join". *Aṣṭāṅga yoga* is a sequence of eight steps:

1️⃣ *Yama*: non-injury, truth, non-stealing, continence, non-grasping.
2️⃣ *Niyama*: purity, contentment, discipline, self-study, surrender to the supreme.
3️⃣ *Āsana*
4️⃣ *Prāṇāyāma*
5️⃣ *Pratyāhāra*: drawing the senses back from outer objects.
6️⃣ *Dhāraṇā*
7️⃣ *Dhyāna*
8️⃣ *Samādhi*

The order matters. Patañjali's claim is that without the ethical ground, the later steps will not give their fruit. When the body is steady, the breath can be guided. When the breath is calm, the mind turns inward.

📖 *दिवस ३१ · Day 31*

*आयुर्वेद*
_औषध आधी, जीवनशैली. सुरुवात ताटातून._

"आयुर्वेद" म्हणजे आयुष्याचं शास्त्र. चरकसंहिता सांगते की जीवन तीन खांबांवर उभं आहे: *आहार, निद्रा आणि ब्रह्मचर्य* (संयम). त्यांना *त्रयोपस्तंभ* म्हणतात.

आयुर्वेदाचा क्रम असा:

१. आधी *आहार* आणि दिनचर्या-ऋतुचर्या.
२. ते पुरेसं नसेल तर *औषधी वनस्पती*.
३. तेही पुरेसं नसेल तर *पंचकर्म* ही शुद्धीची उपचारपद्धती.
४. शेवटी *रसायन*: शरीर पुन्हा भरून काढणारी योजना.

या शास्त्राचं मोजमाप असं की किती कमी लोकांना तीव्र उपचार लागतात. मूलभूत संकल्पना: *त्रिदोष* (वात, पित्त, कफ) आणि सात *धातू*. अन्नातून पहिला धातू, रस, तयार होतो, आणि तो पुढच्या धातूला जन्म देतो.

आयुर्वेदाचा दुसरा मोठा वारसा शस्त्रक्रियेचा. सुश्रुतसंहितेत शस्त्रक्रियेची साधनं आणि पद्धती मांडलेल्या आहेत. नाकाची पुनर्रचना हा त्यातला प्रसिद्ध उल्लेख आहे.

📖 *Day 31*

*Āyurveda*
_Lifestyle first, medicine after. It starts on your plate._

"Āyurveda" means the science of life. The Caraka-saṃhitā says life stands on three pillars: *food, sleep and self-restraint*, the *trayopastambha*.

The order of Āyurvedic care runs like this:

1️⃣ First, *food* and a good daily and seasonal routine.
2️⃣ If that is not enough, *medicinal herbs*.
3️⃣ If still not enough, the cleansing therapies of *pañcakarma*.
4️⃣ Finally, *rasāyana*: rejuvenation to rebuild what illness and time have worn away.

The system measures itself by how few people need the sharper treatments. Its core ideas are the *tridoṣa* (Vāta, Pitta, Kapha) and the seven *dhātus*, the tissues. Digested food first becomes rasa, and each tissue gives rise to the next.

Āyurveda has a second great legacy in surgery. The Suśruta-saṃhitā describes surgical instruments and methods, including the well-known account of reconstructing a nose.

📖 *Day 32*

*The Six Āstika Darśanas*
_Six ways of seeing. They argue with one another, yet share one house._

The word "darśana" means seeing. These are six ways of looking at the world and the self. They are called *āstika* because they accept the authority of the Veda. (This does not mean "believer in God". More on that later.)

The six are grouped in three pairs, where one member usually builds the theory and the other the practice or its counterpart:

1️⃣ *Nyāya* and *Vaiśeṣika*: logic, and the analysis of what exists.
2️⃣ *Sāṅkhya* and *Yoga*: a theory of consciousness and nature, and the discipline built on it.
3️⃣ *Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā* and *Vedānta*: reading the ritual part and the knowledge part of the Veda.

They argued fiercely with one another. But the discipline of the debate required stating your opponent's view fairly before refuting it. That is how each school developed its own logic, vocabulary and questions.

A tip while reading: first see which question each darśana is asking, and only then its answer.

📖 *Day 33*

*Nyāya*
_The science of arguing well._

You see smoke on a hill and say, "There is fire there." Why? "Because there is smoke." Example? "Like a kitchen: where there is smoke, there is fire." That is the *Nyāya* method, and this is its most famous illustration.

Its founder is traditionally Gautama. His Nyāya-sūtra opens with a list of *sixteen categories*, beginning with pramāṇa (means of knowledge), prameya (the knowable), saṃśaya (doubt), prayojana (purpose), dṛṣṭānta (example) and siddhānta (settled conclusion).

An inference (*anumāna*) has five members:

1️⃣ Claim: the hill has fire.
2️⃣ Reason: because it has smoke.
3️⃣ Example: like a kitchen.
4️⃣ Application: this hill has such smoke too.
5️⃣ Conclusion: therefore it has fire.

Nyāya also catalogued bad reasoning, the *hetvābhāsa* or fallacies. Its spirit was to debate in order to find truth, not merely to win. Nearly every later Indian thinker used its tools.

📖 *Day 34*

*Sāṅkhya*
_The school of enumeration. An inventory of what exists._

A blind man and a lame man are stranded in a forest. The blind man can walk but cannot see. The lame man can see but cannot walk. The lame man climbs on the blind man's shoulders, and together they find the way out. This is an image from the Sāṅkhya-kārikā. The lame man is *puruṣa*, the witnessing consciousness. The blind man is *prakṛti*, active nature.

Sāṅkhya is considered the oldest of the six. Tradition names Kapila as its founder. In the Gītā, Kṛṣṇa says, "siddhānāṃ kapilo muniḥ": among the perfected, I am Kapila.

Sāṅkhya made an inventory of the world: *twenty-five tattvas*. Puruṣa and prakṛti, then mahat (the intellect), ahaṅkāra (ego), mind, the five senses of knowing, the five organs of action, the five subtle elements and the five gross elements.

Prakṛti is woven of three *guṇas*: sattva, rajas and tamas. The same vocabulary runs through the Gītā and Yoga.

The Kārikā is by Īśvarakṛṣṇa, and its roughly seventy verses hold the heart of this whole system.

📖 *Day 35*

*The Yoga Darśana*
_Sāṅkhya says what is real. Yoga says what to do about it._

The second sūtra of Patañjali's Yoga-sūtras is just four words: "yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ". Yoga is the stilling of the modifications of the mind. Put simply: calming the endless waves of the mind.

The work has only *196 sūtras*, in four *pādas*:

1️⃣ *Samādhi-pāda*
2️⃣ *Sādhana-pāda*, where the eight limbs appear
3️⃣ *Vibhūti-pāda*
4️⃣ *Kaivalya-pāda*

Sāṅkhya's groundwork, puruṣa and prakṛti, lies beneath it. But where Sāṅkhya says what is, Yoga shows how to experience it. *Īśvara-praṇidhāna*, surrender to the supreme, appears as one route.

Patañjali knows that calming the mind is not easy. One sūtra (1.12) says it comes through two things together: *abhyāsa and vairāgya*, steady practice and a loosening of attachment.

Yoga, in short, is far larger than gym-style exercise.

📖 *Day 36*

*Vedānta*
_The end of the Veda, and also its goal._

"Vedānta" means the end of the Veda, in two senses: the closing portion of the Veda, the Upaniṣads, and also the Veda's final aim.

Bādarāyaṇa's *Brahma-sūtra* gathers these teachings into aphorisms. It opens: "athāto brahma-jijñāsā", now, therefore, the desire to know Brahman.

The sūtras are so terse that they are hard to follow without explanation, which is why teachers wrote commentaries (*bhāṣya*) on them. Writing on the *prasthāna-trayī*, the Upaniṣads, the Brahma-sūtra and the Bhagavad-gītā, became the way to present one's own Vedānta.

Three major streams emerged:

1️⃣ *Advaita*, Śaṅkara: the self and Brahman are one.
2️⃣ *Viśiṣṭādvaita*, Rāmānuja: one reality, but internally differentiated.
3️⃣ *Dvaita*, Madhva: God, souls and world are distinct and real.

Three thinkers drew three answers from the same texts. How that was possible is what we shall see next.

कणाद ऋषी या दर्शनाचे प्रणेते. त्यांच्या नावाचा संबंध "कण", म्हणजे सूक्ष्म कणाशी जोडला जातो. त्यांच्या मते जग अविभाज्य अणूंपासून बनलं आहे. ग्रीक अणुवादाशी तुलना करता येईल असा हा विचार आहे. (दोघांच्या कालाबद्दल अभ्यासकांत चर्चा आहे.)

सर्व वास्तव त्यांनी *पदार्थ* या वर्गांत मांडलं:

१. द्रव्य (वस्तू)
२. गुण
३. कर्म (क्रिया)
४. सामान्य (समान जात)
५. विशेष
६. समवाय (अविभाज्य संबंध)

पुढे सातवा, *अभाव* (अनुपस्थिती), जोडला गेला.

या ग्रंथाची सुरुवात धर्माच्या विवेचनाने होते. त्यातली व्याख्या अशी की जे अभ्युदय (ऐहिक उन्नती) आणि निःश्रेयस (परम कल्याण) साधतं, तो धर्म.

📖 *Day 37*

*Vaiśeṣika*
_The school of particulars. Atoms and categories._

In a beam of sunlight you can see specks of dust floating. Traditional commentators give this sight a meaning: that speck is the smallest piece we can see, and it is itself made of finer parts. This is the *aṇu*, the atom, the signature idea of the Vaiśeṣika school.

Its founder is Kaṇāda, whose name is linked with *kaṇa*, a tiny particle. He held that the world is built from indivisible atoms. It is a view comparable to Greek atomism, though scholars debate how the two compare in date.

He sorted all reality into *padārthas*, categories:

1️⃣ Substance
2️⃣ Quality
3️⃣ Action
4️⃣ Generality
5️⃣ Particularity
6️⃣ Inherence, the inseparable bond

A seventh, *abhāva* (absence), was added later.

The text opens with a discussion of dharma. It defines dharma as that which brings both abhyudaya (worldly well-being) and niḥśreyasa (the highest good).

*पूर्व-मीमांसा*
_वेदवाक्याचा अर्थ कसा लावायचा, याचं शास्त्र._

📖 *Day 38*

*Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā*
_The science of reading a Vedic sentence correctly._

The Veda has a line: "svarga-kāmo yajeta", one who desires heaven should sacrifice. Now ask: is this a command, or advice? What exactly is "sacrifice"? Under what conditions? Settling such questions with care is the work of *Mīmāṃsā*.

Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* is in twelve books. It opens: "athāto dharma-jijñāsā", now, therefore, the desire to know dharma.

Its distinctive position is that dharma's source is no god but the Veda itself. The Veda is held to be *apauruṣeya*, not composed by any person. With no author, there is no author's error. Most Mīmāṃsakas say there is no need to posit a creator god.

The school built rules for interpreting sentences: commands, injunctions, praise-passages, how to reconcile texts that seem to clash. That is why much of Sanskrit's theory of meaning was shaped here. Jurists of the Dharmaśāstra also used these rules to interpret law.

"Pūrva" means the earlier part of the Veda, the ritual portion. The later part is Vedānta.

📖 *Day 39*

*Vedānta Schools*
_One text, many readings. This is where the real arguments begin._

There is one question: how are the individual soul, the world and Brahman related? And there are many answers.

All the teachers who answer comment on the same three texts: the Upaniṣads, the Brahma-sūtra and the Gītā. Yet their readings differ. The brevity of the sūtras is exactly what made different readings possible.

You will meet:

1️⃣ *Gauḍapāda*: the earliest Advaitin whose work survives. The world is dream-like.
2️⃣ *Advaita*: Śaṅkara (traditionally 8th century). Brahman alone is real.
3️⃣ *Viśiṣṭādvaita*: Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017 to 1137). God, souls and world all real, yet one whole.
4️⃣ *Dvaita*: Madhva (13th century). All three are distinct and real.
5️⃣ *Bhedābheda*: Nimbārka, Vallabha, Caitanya. Difference and non-difference together.

Here is an irony. Advaita calls the world *mithyā*, appearance. Madhva says it is wholly real. Rāmānuja stands between. All three are devout and rigorously logical in their own way.

These debates left a legacy of both knowledge and devotion whose mark is still visible.

📖 *Day 40*

*Gauḍapāda*
_The thinker who said nothing is ever truly born._

In the dark, whirl a glowing stick quickly in a circle. The eye sees a ring of fire. Stop the stick and the ring is gone. This is a famous image from Gauḍapāda's Kārikā (*alāta-śānti*). The ring was never born. Motion only made it seem so.

Gauḍapāda is placed around the sixth century CE, the earliest Advaitin whose work survives. His *Māṇḍūkya-kārikā* sets verse in four chapters on the twelve mantras of the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad. By tradition he is the teacher of Śaṅkara's teacher.

His doctrine is *ajātivāda*: nothing is ever truly born, just as the things in a dream vanish on waking. His best-known verse:

"na nirodho na cotpattir na baddho na ca sādhakaḥ | na mumukṣur na vai mukta ity eṣā paramārthatā ||"

No dissolution, no origination, none bound, none striving, none seeking release, none released. This is the highest truth.

In the fourth chapter he uses arguments close to Buddhist ones, and how exactly he relates to Buddhist thought is still debated.

📖 *Day 41*

*Advaita*
_Ādi Śaṅkara. The self and Brahman are one._

At dusk a rope lies on the road. You take it for a snake. Your heart races. Bring a lamp, and you see that there never was a snake. It is Śaṅkara's favourite illustration. The world seems separate and final to us because we have not recognised Brahman.

Advaita's formula: Brahman is real, the world is *mithyā*, the self is Brahman itself. (Mithyā does not mean "false" so much as "not the final reality".) Like silver that seems to gleam on a shell: real enough in everyday use, not real at the deepest level.

So liberation is not something to be gained but a recognition, when ignorance falls away. And it can happen while still alive, called *jīvanmukti*.

Tradition gives Śaṅkara the years 788 to 820 CE, just thirty-two years. In that time he wrote commentaries on the Upaniṣads, the Brahma-sūtra and the Gītā, and established four monasteries in the four directions: Śṛṅgeri, Dvārakā, Jyotirmaṭha and Puri (Govardhana).

📖 *दिवस ४२ · Day 42*

*विशिष्टाद्वैत*
_रामानुज. एकच तत्त्व, पण आतून वैशिष्ट्यपूर्ण._

तुमचा हात म्हणजे तुम्ही नाही, पण तुमच्यापासून वेगळाही नाही. तो तुमच्यासाठी आणि तुमच्या इच्छेनं चालतो. रामानुजांच्या *शरीर-शरीरी भाव* कल्पनेचा हाच गाभा. सर्व जीव आणि सर्व जड जग हे परमेश्वराचं "शरीर" आहे. ते खरंच आहे, आणि त्याच्याशिवाय नाही.

रामानुज (परंपरेनुसार १०१७ ते ११३७, म्हणजे १२० वर्षं) यांच्यासाठी ब्रह्म म्हणजे निर्गुण तत्त्व नव्हे, तर अनंत कल्याणगुणांचा विष्णू-नारायण. आणि मुक्तीमध्येही जीव आपलं वेगळेपण गमावत नाही. माया ही भ्रम नाही, तर देवाची खरी सर्जनशक्ती.

मार्ग: भक्ती आणि *प्रपत्ती*, म्हणजे पूर्ण शरणागती. गीतेचा "सर्वधर्मान्परित्यज्य मामेकं शरणं व्रज" हा चरमश्लोक त्याचा आधार.

श्री वैष्णव परंपरेतली एक कथा: गुरू तिरुकोट्टियूर नंबी यांनी हा मंत्र गुप्त ठेवायला सांगितला होता. पण रामानुजांनी मंदिराच्या गोपुरावर चढून तो सर्वांना ऐकवला, असं सांगितलं जातं. त्यांचं ग्रंथकार्य *श्रीभाष्य*.

📖 *Day 42*

*Viśiṣṭādvaita*
_Rāmānuja. One reality, differentiated within._

Your hand is not you, yet it is not apart from you. It moves for you and by your will. That is the heart of Rāmānuja's *śarīra-śarīri-bhāva*, the body–soul relation. All souls and all matter are the "body" of the Lord: truly real, and never separate from him.

For Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017 to 1137, a span of 120 years) Brahman is not an attributeless absolute but Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa, full of infinite auspicious qualities. Even in liberation the soul keeps its individuality. Māyā is not illusion but God's real creative power.

The path is bhakti and *prapatti*, complete loving surrender, with the Gītā's carama-śloka, "sarva-dharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja", as its charter.

A tale from the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition: his teacher Tirukkoṭṭiyūr Nambi told him to keep this mantra secret, yet Rāmānuja is said to have climbed the temple tower and shared it with all who gathered. His great work is the *Śrī-Bhāṣya*.

📖 *Day 43*

*Dvaita*
_Madhva. God, souls and world: all real, all distinct._

Madhvācārya (Ānandatīrtha, traditionally 1238 to 1317) held a plain and firm view: what is real is also distinct. So God, souls and world are all eternally real and eternally different.

He set out this difference as the *pañca-bheda*, five differences:

1️⃣ God and soul
2️⃣ God and matter
3️⃣ Soul and soul
4️⃣ Soul and matter
5️⃣ Matter and matter

Brahman is Viṣṇu, the one *svatantra*, independent reality. Everything else is *paratantra*: real, but dependent on him. Liberation is the soul enjoying its own bliss in God's presence.

Madhva composed thirty-seven works and began the tradition at Uḍupi's Kṛṣṇa temple. Uḍupi has a distinctive practice: it has eight monasteries, and the responsibility for worship passes from one to the next every two years in the festival called *Paryāya*.

Of the three great Vedānta schools, this is the most uncompromisingly realist.

📖 *Day 44*

*Bhedābheda Streams*
_Wave and ocean: distinct, and yet one._

Is a wave separate from the ocean? It has its own shape, sound and moment. Yet without the ocean it is nothing. Sparks and fire are the same. The *bhedābheda* tradition says the soul is both different from Brahman and not different. Its earliest systematic exponent is Bhāskara (9th century).

This position makes room for devotion: the devotee stays real enough to love God, yet is never wholly cut off from him.

Three great streams, all leaning towards Kṛṣṇa-bhakti:

1️⃣ *Nimbārka*: Dvaitādvaita. Difference and non-difference are equally real.
2️⃣ *Vallabha*: Śuddhādvaita. The world is a real manifestation of Brahman. His Puṣṭimārga is the path of grace.
3️⃣ *Caitanya*: Acintya-bhedābheda, "inconceivable difference-and-non-difference". Out of it flowered Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism and its saṅkīrtana.

A verse from Caitanya's Śikṣāṣṭaka: "tṛṇād api sunīcena tarror api sahiṣṇunā | amāninā mānadena kīrtanīyaḥ sadā hariḥ ||" Humbler than a blade of grass, more forbearing than a tree, claiming no honour yet giving it to all, one should sing Hari's name always.

📖 *Day 45*

*The Nāstika Darśanas*
_The loyal opposition, whose questions sharpened everyone._

"Nāstika" is often taken to mean "atheist". But the older meaning differs. In Pāṇini's grammar a nāstika is one who holds "nāsti", "(the other world) is not". Later, the Vedic tradition used the word for those who reject the authority of the Veda. So Jains and Buddhists, who accept rebirth, fall in this class too.

You will meet three schools:

1️⃣ *Cārvāka*: only perception (*pratyakṣa*) counts as proof. Its own books are almost entirely lost, so its views are known mainly through its critics.
2️⃣ *Bauddha*: built on the Buddha's teaching.
3️⃣ *Jaina*: Mahāvīra's tradition, of non-violence and *anekānta*.

The Buddha and Mahāvīra taught around the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, in the śramaṇa tradition. Their questions forced Vedic thinkers to make their own positions sharper.

History also records respect between these paths. Emperor Aśoka's rock edicts urge honouring all sects.

📖 *Day 46*

*Cārvāka*
_India’s boldest questioner: only what you can perceive is real._

Imagine a thinker standing up and asking: "Soul, rebirth, heaven. Has anyone actually seen them?" The question is so old that India built a whole school around it: *Cārvāka*, also called *Lokāyata*, "the view of the people".

Its claim is simple. Only *pratyakṣa*, direct perception, counts as proof. The body is the self. Consciousness arises from the four elements (earth, water, fire, air), the way intoxicating power arises when grain ferments.

One sharp question is credited to them, as recorded by their opponents: if an animal killed in a sacrifice goes to heaven, why not sacrifice your own father?

Here is the odd part. No Cārvāka text of its own survives. We know the school only through the refutations written by *its critics*. The people who wanted to defeat these ideas are the ones who preserved them.

"While you live, live happily; even borrow, if you must, to drink ghee."

_रथ कुठे आहे? "मी" कुठे आहे?_

इसवी सनापूर्वी दुसऱ्या शतकातली एक कथा आहे. ग्रीक वंशाचा राजा *मिलिंद* भिक्षू *नागसेन* यांना विचारतो, "तुमचं नाव काय?" नागसेन म्हणतात, "नागसेन हे फक्त नाव आहे, आत कोणी व्यक्ती सापडत नाही." राजा चकित होतो. मग नागसेन विचारतात, "तुम्ही इथे कशातून आलात?" "रथातून." "रथ म्हणजे चाक का? आस का? सांगाडा का?" राजा म्हणतो, "यापैकी कोणीच नाही." "मग रथ कुठे आहे?" हे *मिलिंद-पञ्ह* या पाली ग्रंथात आलेलं प्रसिद्ध उदाहरण आहे.

हाच बौद्ध विचारांचा गाभा आहे: सगळं अनित्य (*अनित्य*), सगळं दुःखमय (*दुःख*), आणि कुठेही कायमचा "स्वतः" नाही (*अनात्म*). सगळं काही एकमेकांवर अवलंबून उगवतं, हा *प्रतीत्यसमुत्पाद*.

सारनाथला बुद्धांनी पहिल्या प्रवचनात चार आर्यसत्यं सांगितली: दुःख आहे, त्याचं कारण आहे, त्याचा अंत शक्य आहे, आणि त्याचा मार्ग आहे.

पुढे या धारेचे थेरवाद, महायान आणि वज्रयान असे प्रवाह झाले. नागार्जुन, असंग आणि वसुबंधू यांसारखे महान विचारवंत यातच घडले.

💭 तुम्ही स्वतःला "मी" म्हणता, तेव्हा नेमकं कोणाला म्हणता: शरीराला, मनाला, आठवणींना की आणखी कशाला?

📖 *Day 47*

*Bauddha (Buddhist School)*
_Where is the chariot? Where is the "I"?_

A famous story comes from a Pali text, the *Milinda-pañha*. The Greek-descended king *Milinda* asks the monk *Nāgasena* his name. "Nāgasena is only a label," says the monk, "no person can be found inside." The king is puzzled. So Nāgasena asks how he travelled there. "By chariot." "Is the chariot the wheel? The axle? The frame?" "None of them." "Then where is the chariot?"

That is the heart of Buddhist thought. All things are impermanent (*anitya*), unsatisfying (*duḥkha*) and without a fixed self (*anātman*). Everything arises in dependence on other things: *pratītya-samutpāda*.

At Sarnath the Buddha gave his first teaching, the Four Noble Truths: there is suffering, it has a cause, it can end, and there is a path to its ending.

Over time the stream divided into *Theravāda*, *Mahāyāna* and *Vajrayāna*. Within Mahāyāna came the great thinkers Nāgārjuna, Asaṅga and Vasubandhu, who argued as sharply with each other as with anyone outside.

💭 When you say "I", what exactly do you mean: the body, the mind, the memories, or something else?

*जैन दर्शन*
_सत्य एका बाजूने दिसतं; पूर्ण सत्य अनेक बाजूंनी._

अनेक जैन साधू आणि साध्वी चालताना आधी जमीन मोरपिसाच्या झाडूने हळूच झाडतात, जेणेकरून पायाखाली एखादा सूक्ष्म जीवही चिरडला जाऊ नये. ही केवळ सवय नाही, तर एका तत्त्वज्ञानाचा आचार आहे.

📖 *Day 48*

*Jaina (Jain School)*
_Truth shows one face at a time; the whole truth has many._

Many Jain monks and nuns gently sweep the ground with a soft broom before they walk, so that no tiny creature is crushed under their feet. It is not just a habit. It is a philosophy put into practice.

*Ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ*: non-injury is the supreme dharma. In Jainism it is to be kept in thought, speech and act. This links to the school's greatest intellectual gift: *anekānta*. Reality has endless aspects, so any statement is true only from a standpoint. That is *syādvāda* (*syāt* meaning "in some respect").

The parable of the blind men and the elephant is often used to explain it. Each touches one part and takes it for the whole. The truth is what all of them together describe.

The world itself is made of two things: *jīva* (the conscious soul) and *ajīva* (matter). The path to liberation has three parts: right view, right knowledge and right conduct. *Mahāvīra* is honoured as the 24th *tīrthaṅkara*, and the tradition later divided into the *Digambara* and *Śvetāmbara* paths.

📖 *Day 49*

*Philosophy of Language*
_The one tradition that made grammar a path to liberation._

Grammar is the dullest subject at school, or so many of us feel. In India it climbed to the summit of philosophy.

*Pāṇini* fitted the whole Sanskrit language into about four thousand compact rules, and scholars treat him as the root of this tradition. *Kātyāyana* and *Patañjali* built on him. The American linguist Leonard Bloomfield called Pāṇini's grammar one of the greatest monuments of human intelligence.

Later *Bhartṛhari*, in his *Vākyapadīya*, made a bold claim: ultimate reality is itself of the nature of the Word, *śabda-brahman*. Meaning is not grasped piece by piece but whole, in one flash, called *sphoṭa*. And correct speech is itself a discipline of the spirit.

Here you will meet Bhartṛhari and the Vākyapadīya, śabda-brahman and sphoṭa, grammar as a path to liberation, Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya*, and the long legacy of the grammarians.

Why does it matter? Because we think in words. The question "what is a word, and what is meaning?" sits underneath every other question.

📖 *Day 50*

*Bhartṛhari & the Vākyapadīya*
_Where the world itself is seen as the appearance of the Word._

अनादिनिधनं ब्रह्म शब्दतत्त्वं यदक्षरम् ।
विवर्ततेऽर्थभावेन प्रक्रिया जगतो यतः ॥

"The Brahman without beginning or end, whose essence is the Word, the imperishable: it appears as objects, and from it the world unfolds." This is the opening verse of the *Vākyapadīya*. Its author *Bhartṛhari* (about the 5th century CE) took the technical grammar of Pāṇini and Patañjali and turned it into metaphysics.

The work has three books: the *Brahma-kāṇḍa* (the Word-Absolute), the *Vākya-kāṇḍa* (the sentence) and the *Pada-kāṇḍa* (words).

One lovely idea is the three levels of speech:
1️⃣ *Vaikharī*: audible, spoken speech.
2️⃣ *Madhyamā*: the word held in the mind.
3️⃣ *Paśyantī*: the "seeing" word, speech at its source, not yet divided.

Before we say anything, there is a vague sense of "what I want to say". This scheme catches the journey from that sense to actual sound. It later travelled deep into Tantra and Kashmir Śaivism.

(This grammarian Bhartṛhari is usually held to be a different person from the poet of the Nīti, Śṛṅgāra and Vairāgya śatakas.)
