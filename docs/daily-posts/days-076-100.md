*महाकाव्य*
_अलंकारांनी मढलेली दरबारी महाकाव्यं._

कालिदासाचं रघुवंश सुरू होतं ते अशा श्लोकानं:

_वागर्थाविव संपृक्तौ वागर्थप्रतिपत्तये ।_
_जगतः पितरौ वन्दे पार्वतीपरमेश्वरौ ॥_
"शब्द आणि अर्थ जसे एकमेकांत मिसळलेले असतात, तसे जोडलेले जगाचे माता-पिता, पार्वती आणि परमेश्वर, यांना मी वंदन करतो."

महाकाव्य म्हणजे अनेक सर्गांचं, एकाच वीरगाथेवर उभं, प्रत्येक ओळ अलंकारांनी भरलेलं दरबारी काव्य. परंपरेनं पाच काव्यं प्रमाण मानली: कालिदासाची दोन (रघुवंश, कुमारसंभव), भारवीचं किरातार्जुनीय, माघाचं शिशुपालवध आणि श्रीहर्षाचं नैषधीयचरित.

माघाच्या काव्यात शब्दांच्या कसरती आहेत. असे श्लोक आहेत जे पुढून आणि मागून सारखे वाचले जातात. श्रीहर्षाचं नैषध इतकं अवघड की त्यावर टीकेशिवाय वाचणं क्वचितच शक्य होतं.

📖 *Day 76*

*Mahākāvya*
_The great court poems, jewelled with figures of speech._

Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa opens with this verse:

_vāg-arthāv iva saṃpṛktau vāg-artha-pratipattaye ।_
_jagataḥ pitarau vande pārvatī-parameśvarau ॥_
"Joined as speech and meaning are joined, I bow to the parents of the world, Pārvatī and Parameśvara, to gain right understanding of speech and meaning."

A mahākāvya is a courtly poem in many cantos, built on a single heroic subject, with ornament in every line. Tradition counts five as canonical: Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa and Kumārasambhava, Bhāravi's Kirātārjunīya, Māgha's Śiśupālavadha and Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita.

Māgha's poem is famous for verbal acrobatics, including verses that read the same forwards and backwards. Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadha is so dense that few readers manage it without a commentary.

Even so, the form kept being composed into the eighteenth century, a remarkable lifespan for any literary genre.

*नाट्य*
_संस्कृत रंगभूमी आणि रसांचा सिद्धांत._

📖 *Day 77*

*Nāṭya*
_Sanskrit theatre and the doctrine of rasa._

In 1789 Sir William Jones published an English translation of Kālidāsa's *Abhijñāna-Śākuntalam*. It is well known that Goethe, reading it in German, was deeply impressed. A single Sanskrit play had stirred the literary world of Europe.

Behind it all lies Bharata's *Nāṭya-śāstra*, regarded as perhaps the oldest surviving treatise on drama anywhere, dated between the second century BCE and the second century CE. It classifies plays into ten forms (daśarūpaka), covers stage, costume and gesture, and sets out the *rasa* doctrine: a play delights the audience by evoking an emotional flavour such as love, heroism, pity or wonder.

The plays include Bhāsa's short, vivid dramas, Śūdraka's urban and comic *Mṛcchakaṭika*, Bhavabhūti's tragic *Uttara-rāma-carita*, and Viśākhadatta's political thriller *Mudrārākṣasa*.

In Sanskrit drama, high-status characters speak Sanskrit, while women and servants speak Prakrit, a linguistic snapshot of the society that produced it.

*खंडकाव्य*
_एक भाव, एक प्रवास. लहान पण खोल काव्य._

📖 *Day 78*

*Khaṇḍa-kāvya*
_One mood, one journey: short poems that run deep._

A yakṣa is cursed by his master to spend a year away from his beloved, stranded on the hills of Rāmagiri. The monsoon arrives, and a cloud drifts overhead. The yakṣa speaks to it: "Carry my message to my wife."

This is Kālidāsa's *Meghadūta*. Using the message as a pretext, the poet traces the cloud's route as a poetic map of northern India. It began a whole genre, the *sandeśa-kāvya*, in which later poets sent geese, parrots and peacocks carrying love-notes.

A khaṇḍa-kāvya is the shorter cousin of the mahākāvya, holding a single mood.

1️⃣ Kālidāsa's *Ṛtusaṃhāra* cycles through six seasons.
2️⃣ Bilhaṇa's *Caurapañcāśikā*, fifty stanzas a condemned poet is said to have composed about his beloved on the night before his execution.
3️⃣ Jayadeva's *Gītagovinda*, twenty-four eight-line songs on Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa.

In such short poems every stanza becomes a complete dramatic moment.

*कथा आणि आख्यायिका*
_गोष्टींनी जगाचा प्रवास केला._

जगात सर्वाधिक भाषांमध्ये भाषांतरित झालेलं भारतीय पुस्तक कोणतं? *पंचतंत्र*.

📖 *Day 79*

*Kathā & Ākhyāyikā*
_Tales that travelled the world._

Which Indian book has been translated into more languages than any other? The *Pañcatantra*.

Tradition says Viṣṇuśarman composed its five books, through animal fables, to teach statecraft to three dull princes. The first book is *Mitra-bheda*, "the splitting of friends", and the text opens: "athedaṃ prathamaṃ tantraṃ mitrabhedo nāma."

In the sixth century it passed into Middle Persian (by Burzōē). From there it reached Arabic as Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's *Kalīla wa Dimna*, named for the two jackals of the story (Karaṭaka and Damanaka), and then Syriac, Greek, Latin and nearly every European language.

Sanskrit prose has a high-literary wing too: Bāṇa's *Kādambarī* and Daṇḍin's *Daśakumāracarita*. The *Hitopadeśa* is Nārāyaṇa's smoother retelling of Pañcatantra material, used for centuries to teach Sanskrit prose in schools.

So the Indian trick of teaching politics through a story was borrowed by the world.

📖 *Day 80*

*The Kāvya Poets*
_The makers behind the genres, from Kālidāsa to Jayadeva._

Sanskrit poetry has its forms: the epic, the play, the prose tale. But forms come alive through poets, and this section is about them.

You will meet:

1️⃣ *Kālidāsa*: the supreme poet, whose simile became a proverb.
2️⃣ *The Dramatists*: Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Viśākhadatta, Bhavabhūti.
3️⃣ *Bāṇabhaṭṭa*: emperor of dense, ornate prose.
4️⃣ *The Mahākāvya Masters*: Bhāravi, Māgha, Śrīharṣa.
5️⃣ *Daṇḍin*: playful storyteller and theorist.
6️⃣ *Jayadeva*: the Gītagovinda of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa.

Behind them all stands one ideal, *rasa*: a poem is not mere information but a flavour that lingers in the heart.

About many of these poets we know very little for certain. Nobody knows Kālidāsa's birthplace or birth year. Yet through their poetry they have kept speaking for some fifteen hundred years.

📖 *दिवस ८१ · Day 81*

*कालिदास*
_संस्कृतातला सर्वश्रेष्ठ कवी. आयुष्य अज्ञात, काव्य अमर._

"उपमा कालिदासस्य", म्हणजे उपमेसाठी कालिदास. हा वाक्प्रचार शतकानुशतकं म्हणीसारखा चालत आला.

कालिदासाबद्दल निश्चित माहिती जवळजवळ नाही. परंपरा त्याला एका विक्रमादित्याच्या दरबारी ठेवते, बहुधा गुप्तसम्राट दुसरा चंद्रगुप्त, आणि काळ साधारण इ.स. चौथं-पाचवं शतक. पण त्याच्या सात रचना टिकल्या आहेत: तीन नाटकं (अभिज्ञानशाकुन्तल, विक्रमोर्वशीय, मालविकाग्निमित्र), दोन महाकाव्यं (रघुवंश, कुमारसंभव) आणि दोन गीतकाव्यं (मेघदूत, ऋतुसंहार).

*अभिज्ञानशाकुन्तल* या नावातच एक गोष्ट दडली आहे. "अभिज्ञान" म्हणजे ओळखीची खूण. राजा दुष्यन्ताने शकुंतलेला दिलेली अंगठी हीच ती खूण, जिच्यामुळे विसरलेली नाती पुन्हा आठवतात.

त्याची खासियत भपका नाही तर समतोल: सहज उपमा, निसर्गाबद्दलची जवळीक आणि भावनांतला संयम. अनुवादातूनही गटेसारख्या कवींना त्याचं काव्य भारून टाकू शकलं, हे त्याच्या सामर्थ्याचं मोठं प्रमाण आहे.

📖 *Day 81*

*Kālidāsa*
_The supreme Sanskrit poet: a life unknown, a poetry undying._

"Upamā Kālidāsasya": for the simile, Kālidāsa. That phrase has lived on as a proverb for centuries.

Almost nothing is certain about his life. Tradition places him at the court of a King Vikramāditya, probably the Gupta emperor Candragupta II, around the fourth or fifth century CE. But seven works survive: three plays (Abhijñānaśākuntala, Vikramorvaśīya, Mālavikāgnimitra), two epics (Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava) and two lyrics (Meghadūta, Ṛtusaṃhāra).

A story hides inside the title *Abhijñānaśākuntala*. *Abhijñāna* means a token of recognition. It is the ring King Duṣyanta gave Śakuntalā, the sign through which a forgotten bond is remembered.

His signature is balance, not show: effortless similes, closeness to nature and restraint in feeling. That even translations could move poets like Goethe is a great testimony to his power.

*नाटककार*
_भास, शूद्रक, विशाखदत्त, भवभूती. रंगभूमीवरचे चार वेगळे आवाज._

📖 *Day 82*

*The Dramatists*
_Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Viśākhadatta, Bhavabhūti: four voices of the stage._

In 1912, thirteen Sanskrit plays turned up in manuscripts from the south (the Travancore-Kerala region) and astonished scholars. They were by Bhāsa, one of the earliest Sanskrit dramatists whose work survives. The finest is thought to be the *Svapnavāsavadatta*.

Śūdraka's *Mṛcchakaṭika* means "the little clay cart". It has an impoverished merchant, a courtesan heroine, and a city full of gamblers and thieves. It is considered the warmest and most lifelike of the ancient plays.

Viśākhadatta's *Mudrārākṣasa* is pure politics: Cāṇakya's contest of strategy with the minister Rākṣasa, with scarcely a woman in it.

Bhavabhūti (eighth century) is the strongest dramatist after Kālidāsa. His *Uttararāmacarita* is a tragedy of the separation of Rāma and Sītā. Elsewhere he proudly declares that someone of kindred taste will one day be born, for time is boundless and the earth is wide. That verse is still quoted today.

📖 *Day 83*

*Bāṇabhaṭṭa*
_The emperor of Sanskrit art-prose._

"Bāṇocchiṣṭaṃ jagat sarvam": "the whole world is Bāṇa's leftovers." It is the tradition's tribute: Bāṇa handled so many themes that nothing fresh was left for later poets.

Bāṇabhaṭṭa served at the court of Emperor Harṣa of Kanauj in the seventh century. His *Kādambarī* is a romance of lovers across rebirths. It was left unfinished, and tradition says his son completed it. Its sentences are so long, so full of compounds and double meanings, that a single description can run for pages.

His other book, the *Harṣacarita*, tells of Harṣa and is the first Sanskrit prose biography of a living historical king. Its opening gives the poet's own village, family and restless youth, with surprising frankness.

Kālidāsa is great in restraint, Bāṇa in lavish ornament. Two opposite beauties, and the tradition honoured both.

📖 *Day 84*

*The Mahākāvya Masters*
_Bhāravi, Māgha, Śrīharṣa: the test after Kālidāsa._

There is a famous Sanskrit verse:

_upamā kālidāsasya bhāraver artha-gauravam ।_
_daṇḍinaḥ pada-lālityaṃ māghe santi trayo guṇāḥ ॥_
"For simile, Kālidāsa; for weight of meaning, Bhāravi; for grace of phrase, Daṇḍin; but in Māgha all three virtues meet."

It is a playful traditional scale for ranking poets.

Bhāravi (sixth century) wrote the *Kirātārjunīya*, the combat of Arjuna with Śiva disguised as a hunter. It is prized for the gravity and density of its meaning.

Māgha (seventh century) answered with the *Śiśupālavadha*, consciously built to display every excellence, including a famous canto of verbal and metrical acrobatics.

Śrīharṣa (twelfth century) wrote the *Naiṣadhīyacarita* on Nala and Damayantī, so learned that few can follow it without a commentary. It was called the "scholar's poem".

With Kālidāsa's two, these make the five canonical mahākāvyas, the form on which a poet was finally judged.

📖 *Day 85*

*Daṇḍin & Prose Romance*
_The storyteller and the theorist: the most readable Sanskrit prose._

One verse of Daṇḍin's *Kāvyādarśa* is famous:

_idam andhantamaḥ kṛtsnaṃ jāyeta bhuvanatrayam ।_
_yadi śabdāhvayaṃ jyotir āsaṃsāraṃ na dīpyate ॥_
"This whole three-world would be blind darkness if the light called 'the word' did not shine from the beginning of creation."

Daṇḍin (about the seventh or eighth century) is great in two roles.

First, the storyteller. His *Daśakumāracarita* tells the adventures of ten princes through a city of gamblers, conmen, courtesans and merchants, fast and witty. It is far easier than Bāṇa's dense prose.

Second, the theorist. The *Kāvyādarśa*, "mirror of poetry", is one of the earliest manuals of poetics, classifying figures of speech, qualities and styles (mārga).

Beside him stands Subandhu's *Vāsavadattā*, built wholly on *śleṣa*, double meaning. From clear narrative to pure wordplay, these are the two poles of Sanskrit prose.

*जयदेव*
_जेव्हा संस्कृत काव्य गाणं झालं आणि प्रेम भक्ती झालं._

ढगांनी भरलेलं आभाळ, तमालवृक्षांनी काळवंडलेलं वन, आणि रात्रीला घाबरणारा कान्हा. "राधे, याला घरी घेऊन जा." *गीतगोविंद* या काव्याची सुरुवात अशी एखाद्या चित्रासारखी होते.

📖 *Day 86*

*Jayadeva*
_When Sanskrit poetry turned into song, and love turned into devotion._

Clouds hang heavy in the sky, the forest is dark with tamāla trees, and the night frightens little Kṛṣṇa. "Rādhā, take him home." The *Gīta-govinda* opens like a painting.

*Jayadeva*, a poet at the court of king Lakṣmaṇasena in twelfth-century Bengal, composed it. Its twenty-four songs are *aṣṭapadīs*, "eight-footed" lyrics in soft, lilting metres with refrains. They were meant to be sung, not just read. Through the love, separation and reunion of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa, the poem tells of the soul's journey to God.

Here is something you may not expect: two hymns of Jayadeva are also included in the Sikh scripture, the *Guru Granth Sāhib*. And at the Jagannātha temple in Puri, the Gīta-govinda is still sung to the deity.

*Bhakti Traditions*
_The stories turned into song, and God began to speak every language._

The scriptures were in Sanskrit, the mantras on the lips of priests. So how was an ordinary person to speak to God? The bhakti movement answered that question.

It began in the Tamil country around the seventh century and rolled northward over the next thousand years. Its marks are clear: poetry in the people's own languages, a personal relationship with the Lord, and respect for every singer whatever their caste. 📖 *Day 88*

*The Ālvārs*
_Twelve Tamil saints who sang Viṣṇu in their own language._

Between the sixth and ninth centuries, twelve poet-saints walked the Tamil country from one Viṣṇu temple to the next, singing as they went. They are the *Ālvārs*, "those immersed" in God.

About four thousand of their verses were gathered in the tenth century by *Nāthamuni* as the *Nālāyira Divya-prabandham*. Śrīvaiṣṇavas honour it as the Tamil counterpart of the Veda.

Among the twelve is one woman, *Āṇṭāḷ*. Her thirty verses are the *Tiruppāvai*. Tradition says she was found as a baby among tulasi plants, and that she would wear the garland meant for the Lord before it was offered. Even today, in the month of Mārkaḻi (December to January), her verses are sung at dawn in temples and homes across south India.

In the eleventh century Rāmānuja gave this devotion its philosophical form.

*The Nāyaṉārs*
_Sixty-three Tamil devotees of Śiva, and their singing feet._

In the very centuries when the Ālvārs wandered singing of Viṣṇu, devotees of Śiva were walking the same roads. They are the *Nāyaṉārs*, and there are *sixty-three* of them.

Their hymns fill the twelve books of the *Tirumuṟai*. The first seven are the *Tēvāram*, by three poets: *Appar*, who began as a Jain and became a devotee of Śiva; *Sambandar*, the boy-saint; and *Sundarar*, remembered as the friend of the Lord.

*The Northern Sants*
_A weaver, a leather-worker, a princess, all calling the same formless Lord._

Kabīr was a weaver. Ravidās worked with leather. Nāmdev was a tailor. Mīrā was a princess. Tulsīdās was a brahmin. Their birth, trade and region differed, yet all sang in the language of the people: Hindi, Avadhī, Braj, Marathi, Punjabi. Not Sanskrit.

*Gauḍīya & Vārkarī*
_Bengal's saṃkīrtana and Maharashtra's vāri: God walks the streets._

📖 *दिवस ९२ · Day 92*

*आगम आणि तंत्र*

📖 *Day 92*

*Āgamas & Tantra*
_The working manuals behind the temple and the inner path._

The Vedas speak of ritual around a fire. So where do the temple image, its daily worship and the inner sanctum come from? The answer lies in the *Āgamas* and *Tantras*.

These are three vast streams: *Śaiva*, *Vaiṣṇava* and *Śākta*. Much of what happens in a temple, how it is built, how an image is made, how it is installed and worshipped each day, follows rules set down in such texts. In the Vedas a hymn is heard. The word "tantra" carries many misunderstandings. In fact these texts hold mantra, meditation, the study of the body, philosophy and art.

📖 *Day 93*

*Śaiva-āgama*
_Śiva's stream: the temple of the south and the philosophy of Kashmir._

Of the three streams, the Śaiva Āgamas are the oldest and best organised. They run in two main channels.

The first is *Śaiva-siddhānta* of the Tamil south. It holds God and soul to be distinct. Its twenty-eight Āgamas still underlie worship in many Śiva temples of south India.

The second is the *Trika* of Kashmir, which holds the soul and Śiva to be one. Its *Spanda* and *Pratyabhijñā* schools rank among the subtlest philosophy in the Indian record.

The first aphorism of the *Śiva-sūtra* is just two words: *caitanyam ātmā*, "the Self is consciousness." The one in you who knows, it says, is Śiva.

Two roads to the same Śiva: one goes through the temple, the other through the mind.

📖 *Day 97*

*Śiva-sūtra*
_Aphorisms found on a rock, and a whole philosophy in two words._

Tradition says that in the ninth century the sage *Vasugupta* was told in a dream by Śiva that certain aphorisms lay inscribed on a rock on Mount Mahādeva near Srinagar. He went, and found *seventy-seven sūtras*.

These are the *Śiva-sūtras*, the root text of monistic Kashmir Śaivism. The very first aphorism is *caitanyam ātmā*: "The Self is consciousness." Consciousness is not a quality the Self has, it is its very nature, and it is Śiva.

Three sections describe three paths (*upāyas*):
1️⃣ *Śāmbhava*: direct recognition, the immediate way.
2️⃣ *Śākta*: contemplation of mantra and thought.
3️⃣ *Āṇava*: breath, body and concentration.

From this single seed grew both the *Spanda* doctrine and the *Pratyabhijñā* philosophy.

*स्पंद* म्हणजे हालचाल नव्हे. ते चैतन्याचं अंतर्गत सृजनशील स्पंदन आहे, ज्यातून एकच सत्य जग बनतं आणि पुन्हा स्वतःत सामावतं. सुमारे बावन्न श्लोकांचा हा ग्रंथ वसुगुप्त किंवा त्यांचे शिष्य कल्लट यांचा मानला जातो.

यातली शिकवण अतिशय व्यावहारिक आहे. आपल्या अनुभवातले तीव्र क्षण, अचानक भीती, आश्चर्य, झोपेच्या काठावरची अवस्था, किंवा एखाद्या तीव्र इच्छेने मन भारून जाणं, हे स्पंदाच्या *संधी* आहेत. त्या क्षणी विचारांचा पडदा हलका होतो आणि आतलं स्पंदन जाणवतं.

ते पकडून त्यात स्थिर होणं, म्हणजेच स्वतःला शिव म्हणून ओळखणं.

💭 एखाद्या अचानक क्षणी तुमचं मन क्षणभर पूर्ण स्तब्ध झालं आहे का?

📖 *Day 98*

*Spanda-kārikā*
_The pulse of consciousness, and the moments when it can be caught._

The world opens and closes like the blink of an eye. That is how the Spanda-kārikā begins: we praise Śaṅkara, by whose *unmeṣa and nimeṣa*, the opening and closing of the eyes, the world arises and dissolves.

*Spanda* is not physical motion. It is the inner creative throb of consciousness, by which the one reality becomes the world and takes it back. The text, about fifty verses long, is attributed to Vasugupta or to his pupil Kallaṭa.

Its teaching is strikingly practical. The charged moments of experience, a sudden fright, wonder, the edge of sleep, or a strong desire seizing the mind, are *junctures* (*sandhi*). In those gaps the veil of thought thins and the underlying pulse shows through.

To catch that pulse and rest in it is to recognise oneself as Śiva.

💭 Has a sudden moment ever left your mind completely still, if only for a second?

*विज्ञान भैरव तंत्र*
_अनंताकडे नेणारी ११२ दारं._

एकदा देवीने भैरवाला विचारलं: "तुझं खरं स्वरूप काय? विधी आणि रूपांच्या पलीकडे काय आहे?" भैरवाने तत्त्वज्ञान सांगितलं नाही. त्याने *एकशे बारा धारणा* दिल्या, म्हणजे छोट्या ध्यानपद्धती.

📖 *Day 99*

*Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra*
_112 gateways to the infinite._

Once the Goddess asked Bhairava: "What is your true nature, beyond ritual and form?" He did not reply with doctrine. He gave her *one hundred and twelve dhāraṇās*, short meditation techniques.

The techniques are strikingly simple: a single breath, the space between two thoughts, the pleasure of a favourite meal, a sneeze, the darkness behind closed eyes. Any ordinary moment can become a doorway to limitless consciousness.

One famous verse says: *wherever the mind goes, outward or inward, there is the state of Śiva. Being all-pervading, where could it go to escape him?*

No temple, image or elaborate rite is needed, only attention rightly placed. That openness is why it remains one of the most widely read of all tantras.

💭 Which ordinary moment today could you meet with your full attention?

*प्रत्यभिज्ञा आणि तंत्रालोक*
_मोक्ष मिळवायचा नसतो, ओळखायचा असतो._

📖 *Day 100*

*Pratyabhijñā & Tantrāloka*
_Liberation is not to be won, but recognised._

Have you ever recognised an old friend in an instant after many years? That is *pratyabhijñā*, "recognition." In the tenth century *Utpaladeva* taught that liberation is the same. We are already Śiva, full and free. Only the recognition has gone missing. Liberation is not gaining something new but remembering what was always so.

The summit of this tradition is *Abhinavagupta* (c. 950–1016). His vast *Tantrāloka* gathered the scattered Śaiva and Śākta scriptures into one ordered system. His disciple *Kṣemarāja* compressed the philosophy into twenty sūtras, the *Pratyabhijñā-hṛdaya*, whose first sūtra is *citiḥ svatantrā*: "Consciousness is free."

Here is something few know: Abhinavagupta also wrote the *Abhinavabhāratī*, a commentary on the Nāṭyaśāstra, and it remains a key source for understanding the theory of *rasa*.
