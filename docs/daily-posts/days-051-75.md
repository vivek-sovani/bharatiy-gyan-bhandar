*शब्दब्रह्म आणि स्फोट*
_अर्थ अक्षरांनी येत नाही; अक्षरांनंतर एका झलकेत उमगतो._

"गाय" हा शब्द ऐकताना तुम्ही काय ऐकता? प्रथम "गा", मग "य". पण "गा" ऐकू येईपर्यंत "य" आलेलाच नसतो, आणि "य" येताच "गा" विरलेला असतो. तरी तुम्हाला अर्थ एकाच वेळी, संपूर्ण उमगतो. हे कसं?

भर्तृहरींचं उत्तर: अर्थ त्या विरणाऱ्या ध्वनींमध्ये राहत नाही. ध्वनी हळूहळू एका अखंड अर्थपूर्णतेला प्रकट करतात. तिला म्हणतात *स्फोट* ("उसळून प्रकट होणारा"). शेवटचा ध्वनी संपताच ती मनात झळाळून येते. त्या झलकेला *प्रतिभा* म्हणतात.

त्यांची दुसरी कल्पना *शब्दाद्वैत*:

न सोऽस्ति प्रत्ययो लोके यः शब्दानुगमादृते ।

📖 *Day 51*

*Śabda-brahman & Sphoṭa*
_Meaning does not come letter by letter; it flashes whole when the sounds end._

What do you actually hear when someone says "gauḥ" (cow)? First "g", then "au", then "ḥ". But when "au" arrives, "g" has already vanished. Yet you grasp one complete meaning. How?

Bhartṛhari's answer: the meaning does not live in the vanishing sounds. They gradually reveal a single, partless whole of meaning, called *sphoṭa* ("bursting forth"). When the last sound falls, it bursts upon the mind in a flash of understanding, the *pratibhā*.

His second idea is *śabdādvaita*:

न सोऽस्ति प्रत्ययो लोके यः शब्दानुगमादृते ।
अनुविद्धमिव ज्ञानं सर्वं शब्देन भासते ॥

"There is no cognition in the world free of the word; all knowledge shines as if interwoven with language." Language, in other words, is not laid over thought from outside. It is the very medium of thought.

The sphoṭa doctrine was contested for centuries by the Mīmāṃsakas and Naiyāyikas, in one of the deepest debates in Indian philosophy.

💭 When you hear a sentence, do you understand it word by word, or does the meaning arrive all at once at the end?

📖 *Day 52*

*Grammar as a Path to Mokṣa*
_Purify the word and you purify the mind._

Liberation through grammar? It sounds odd, but India's grammarians argued it in all seriousness.

तद्द्वारमपवर्गस्य वाङ्मलानां चिकित्सितम् ।
पवित्रं सर्वविद्यानामधिविद्यं प्रकाशते ॥ (Vākyapadīya 1.14)

"It is the doorway to liberation, the cure for the impurities of speech, the purifier of all knowledge: the supreme science shines forth."

The reasoning is direct. If reality is of the nature of the Word, then to know and use words rightly is to come into accord with reality itself. That is why the grammarians took the line between the correct word (*śabda*) and the corrupt form (*apaśabda*) so seriously. It was not pedantry but a kind of spiritual hygiene.

Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* even has a story: the *asuras* were defeated because they mispronounced a word in battle, saying "helayo" for "he 'rayaḥ". A slip of speech becomes a lost war.

Turned inward, the same discipline is *śabda-pūrva-yoga*: resting in the Word that underlies all speech. Grammar becomes yoga.

📖 *Day 53*

*Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya*
_The "great commentary" that argues philosophy out of grammar._

The book opens with the words *atha śabdānuśāsanam*: "Now, the exposition of words." Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* (about the 2nd century BCE) is the commentary on Pāṇini's rules and Kātyāyana's critical notes. The three together are the *trimuni*, "three sages", and in grammar the later authority outranks the earlier.

But it is no dry gloss. It is written as a lively dialogue of objection and reply. It also carries glimpses of its own age. Scholars point to a passage about performing a sacrifice for Puṣyamitra, and another about the Greeks besieging Sāketa, which help date it.

Its first section, the *Paspaśāhnika*, asks why one should study grammar at all. The answers argue that word and meaning are bound eternally (*nitya*), and that śabda is permanent rather than made. And here is the famous line:

एकः शब्दः सम्यग्ज्ञातः सुप्रयुक्तः स्वर्गे लोके च कामधुग् भवति ।
"A single word, perfectly understood and rightly used, becomes a wish-granting cow in heaven and on earth."

The seeds of Bhartṛhari's philosophy are already here.

*वैयाकरणांचा वारसा*
_एका ध्वनीमुळे "स्वजन" "श्वजन" होऊ शकतो._

📖 *Day 54*

*The Grammarian Legacy*
_One sound can turn a kinsman into a dog._

यद्यपि बहु नाधीषे तथापि पठ पुत्र व्याकरणम् ।
स्वजनो श्वजनो मा भूत् सकलं शकलं सकृत् शकृत् ॥

An old subhāṣita says: "My son, even if you study little else, study grammar, lest *svajana* (kinsman) become *śvajana* (a dog), *sakala* (whole) become *śakala* (a fragment), *sakṛt* (once) become *śakṛt* (dung)." It is a light-hearted reminder of how much can hang on a single sound.

Yet the grammarians' philosophy did not stop with Bhartṛhari. In the 17th and 18th centuries it was reborn as *navya-vyākaraṇa*, the "new grammar", through *Kauṇḍa Bhaṭṭa* and above all *Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa*. Nāgeśa restated the theory of sphoṭa with fresh rigour against the dominant Navya-Nyāya logicians.

And the ideas travelled far beyond grammar. The *dhvani* (suggestion) theory of poetics, the *vivarta* model of Advaita Vedānta and the mantra-theory of Tantra and Kashmir Śaivism all carry their mark. Pāṇini's method is also believed to have inspired students of Western linguistics.

📖 *Day 55*

*Itihāsa: "Thus It Was"*
_Where doctrine arrives dressed as narrative._

The word *itihāsa* literally means *iti ha āsa*: "thus indeed it was." In the Indian tradition it names two great epics: Vālmīki's *Rāmāyaṇa* and Vyāsa's *Mahābhārata*.

They are often called "the people's Veda". The Vedas did not reach everyone, but these stories reached every home. The Mahābhārata itself contains a well-known line: *itihāsa-purāṇābhyāṃ vedaṃ samupabṛṃhayet*, "one should expand the meaning of the Veda through itihāsa and purāṇa."

What sets these tales apart is that teaching does not arrive as a lecture. It comes through events: a prince goes into exile, brothers fight brothers, and every character stands before the hard question "what is dharma, really?" Ethics, politics and the householder's dilemmas are all told as story.

In this section you will meet both epics, the *Rāmāyaṇa* and the *Mahābhārata*. Two stories, retold again and again in every Indian language and as far as Southeast Asia.

📖 *Day 56*

*Rāmāyaṇa*
_The birth of the first poem: a verse born from grief._

Tradition tells how the sage *Vālmīki* was walking by the river Tamasā when a hunter shot one of a pair of krauñca birds. Moved by grief, Vālmīki found a verse rising to his lips:

मा निषाद प्रतिष्ठां त्वमगमः शाश्वतीः समाः ।
यत्क्रौञ्चमिथुनादेकमवधीः काममोहितम् ॥

"O hunter, may you find no lasting peace, for you have killed one of this pair of birds." A verse born of sorrow (*śoka* became *śloka*). That is why this is the *ādi-kāvya*, the first poem, and Vālmīki the *ādi-kavi*.

The epic itself opens with a question. Vālmīki asks the sage Nārada, "Who in this world today is virtuous and mighty?" The answer is Rāma. In seven *kāṇḍas* and about 24,000 verses comes his story: the exile, the abduction of Sītā, the war at Laṅkā and the return.

The tale has given many languages their poets: *Tulsīdās* in Avadhī, *Kamban* in Tamil. It lives on in the shadow-puppets of Bali and in Thailand's *Ramakien*.

📖 *Day 57*

*Mahābhārata*
_What is not here is nowhere._

The Mahābhārata is the largest epic ever composed: over a hundred thousand verses in eighteen *parvas*, roughly eight times the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* combined.

According to tradition, Vyāsa composed it in his mind but needed a scribe. Gaṇeśa agreed, on one condition: Vyāsa must never pause in dictation. Vyāsa set his own condition: Gaṇeśa must understand each verse before writing it.

The epic proudly says of itself that whatever is found here may be found elsewhere, and what is not here is nowhere. It deals with dharma, wealth, desire and liberation alike. The whole *Bhagavad-gītā* sits inside it, and so does the *Yakṣa-praśna*. That is why some call it the *pañcama-veda*, the fifth Veda.

यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः ।
"Where dharma is, there is victory." These words are also the motto of the Supreme Court of India.

The story is the war of the Kuru cousins. But the real question is simpler: what is worth fighting for?

📖 *Day 58*

*The Purāṇas*
_Where the old stories kept growing._

*Purāṇa* means "ancient". Eighteen great Purāṇas and eighteen minor ones together form a kind of people's Veda, told, expanded and recomposed over more than a thousand years.

Tradition expects each Purāṇa to treat five subjects: *sarga* (creation), *pratisarga* (dissolution and re-creation), *vaṃśa* (genealogies of gods and sages), *manvantaras* (the ages of the Manus) and *vaṃśānucarita* (histories of royal dynasties). It is, in effect, an encyclopaedia of how the world was made, how it dissolves, and who our ancestors were.

In the classical period the eighteen were sorted into three groups by the guṇa said to predominate: *Vaiṣṇava* (sattva), *Brāhma* (rajas) and *Śaiva* (tamas). The grouping is understood as an arrangement by main deity, not a ranking of gods.

In this section you will meet Viṣṇu's six (led by the highly influential Bhāgavata), the six linked to Brahmā (including the *Devī-māhātmya*), and Śiva's six (including the Skanda, the longest of all).

Through these tales, the stories behind our deities, festivals, pilgrimage places and customs come to life.

📖 *Day 59*

*Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas*
_Six Purāṇas, and the wellspring of the many streams of bhakti._

A king is cursed: you will die in seven days. *Parīkṣit* gives up everything and sits by the Gaṅgā. The young sage *Śuka* arrives and recites the *Bhāgavata* to him for seven days. This, by tradition, is the frame of the Bhāgavata. It answers a hard question: what does a person most want to hear when death is near?

The Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas are six: Viṣṇu, Bhāgavata, Nāradīya, Garuḍa, Padma and Varāha, all treating Viṣṇu as the supreme deity. The most influential of all eighteen is the *Bhāgavata-purāṇa*. Its idea of the *avatāra*, God descending to earth in times of trouble, is the most familiar story in all of bhakti.

📖 *Day 60*

*Brāhma Purāṇas*
_Six Purāṇas, and the book of Navarātri._

During Navarātri, homes and temples recite one text: the *Devī-māhātmya*, also called the *Durgā-saptaśatī*. Seven hundred verses in thirteen chapters. Three stories are best known: the slaying of Madhu and Kaiṭabha, the defeat of Mahiṣāsura, and the war with Śumbha and Niśumbha.

It is part of the *Mārkaṇḍeya-purāṇa*. The six Purāṇas of the Brāhma group are Brahma, Brahmāṇḍa, Brahmavaivarta, Mārkaṇḍeya, Bhaviṣya and Vāmana. This middle group centres on Brahmā, with rajas said to predominate.

Two more things stand out. The *Brahmāṇḍa-purāṇa* contains the *Lalitā-sahasranāma*, a hymn of the Goddess's thousand names. And the *Bhaviṣya-purāṇa* preserves a late layer of material on what the tradition called "future" history.

A famous verse of the Devī-māhātmya:
या देवी सर्वभूतेषु शक्तिरूपेण संस्थिता ।
"She who abides in all beings as power: salutation to her."

Here *śakti* is not only the strength of battle. It is the energy and awareness present in every living being.

*Śaiva Purāṇas*
_Six Purāṇas, five syllables._

ॐ नमः शिवाय ।

This five-syllable mantra (*pañcākṣara*) is the core of the Śaiva tradition. Tradition also links it with the five elements. The words already appear in the Rudra hymn of the Yajurveda.

The six Purāṇas of this group are Śiva, Liṅga, Skanda, Agni, Matsya and Kūrma. They treat Śiva as supreme, with tamas said to predominate.

The *Skanda-purāṇa* is the longest of all eighteen, traditionally about 81,000 verses. It describes pilgrimage places and shrines, such as Kāśī, in great detail. The *Liṅga-purāṇa* sets out the metaphysics of Śaiva-Siddhānta.

*Dharma-śāstra*
_The old law-books on how a society was meant to hold together._

What if a neighbour borrows money and never repays it? How should an inheritance be divided? How much tax may a king take? The texts that answered such questions in India are the *Dharma-śāstras*.

They are *smṛti*, human compositions rather than the "heard" revelation of śruti. They translate dharma into a working civil law for the householder, the king and the court. Four codes stand out: the *Manusmṛti*, *Yājñavalkya-smṛti*, *Nārada-smṛti* and *Bṛhaspati-smṛti*.

The largest, the Manusmṛti, ran for centuries as the operating manual of brahmanical society. The others tightened it, argued with it and updated it. When Sir William Jones translated it into English in 1794, it drew the world's attention.

One point needs honesty. Some of the rules reflect their own time and are strongly debated today. Reading them as historical documents is the most useful way to approach them.

📖 *Day 63*

*Manusmṛti*
_The most cited and the most contested of the dharma-texts._

How does this book present itself? According to tradition, it was taught by *Manu*, the first human, and passed on through the sage Bhṛgu. It has twelve chapters and about 2,694 verses (scholars date it roughly from the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE). From the origin of the world to the soul's journey after death, it lays out conduct for the student, householder, king, judge, merchant and renunciate.

What are the sources of dharma? The text itself says: the Veda, the smṛti, the conduct of the good, and *ātmanas tuṣṭi*, the contentment of one's own heart. The idea of a dharma the heart itself approves appears right at the start.

Yet it is also the most contested of the dharma-texts. The chapters on the duties of the four *varṇas* have been used by defenders of caste society as their authority, and in 1927 Dr. Ambedkar publicly burned the book.

That is why it must be read seriously: to understand how that society saw itself, and to understand what Indian reform movements argued against.

📖 *Day 64*

*Yājñavalkya-smṛti*
_The tidy, lawyerly code._

Manu mixes everything together. The Yājñavalkya-smṛti sorts the field cleanly into three:

1️⃣ *Ācāra*: daily conduct, rites of passage, householder duties.
2️⃣ *Vyavahāra*: legal procedure, witnesses, debt, sale, inheritance.
3️⃣ *Prāyaścitta*: remedies for wrongdoing. Milder than the corresponding chapters in Manu, and it separates deliberate faults from accidental ones.

The layout was easier to consult, and by the seventh century the text had largely displaced Manu in the courtrooms of upper India. It has about 1,000 verses and scholars place it around the 3rd to 5th century CE. Its list of heirs includes the widow and daughters.

Its real power came through the *Mitākṣarā* commentary. In the early 12th century *Vijñāneśvara*, working at the Cālukya court of Kalyāṇa, turned Yājñavalkya's rules on partition and inheritance into the operative law of large parts of the subcontinent. In the Mitākṣarā tradition a son has a right in ancestral property by birth.

This law governed inheritance across most of India until the Hindu Succession Act of 1956.

📖 *Day 65*

*Nārada-smṛti*
_A code written for the courtroom, made for real use._

Have you ever relied on a signed agreement or a witnessed document? The Nārada-smṛti was among the first texts to treat the written contract as a separate category of evidence.

It is the most lawyerly of the smṛtis. Manu and Yājñavalkya cover the householder's whole life, but Nārada is almost entirely about what happens in court. Composed around the 5th century CE, it sets out *eighteen titles of law* (*vyavahāra-padas*): debt-recovery, deposit, partnership, sale, theft, assault, defamation, inheritance and more, each with its own procedure, rules of evidence and sanctions.

Earlier texts treated a witness's spoken testimony as primary and writing as a mere aid. Nārada inverts that: a properly drawn written contract, attested by witnesses, takes precedence. His rules of witness law go into fine detail: who may testify, who may not, and what disqualifies a witness.

He also records the *divine* modes of proof, such as ordeal by fire or water, but limits them to cases where ordinary human evidence has failed.

The text is short, sharp and practical, and was probably composed for active use.

📖 *Day 66*

*Bṛhaspati-smṛti*
_Which proof weighs more? An old ladder of evidence._

Two neighbours quarrel over a plot of land. Each says, "It is mine." How should a judge decide?

Bṛhaspati gives a clear answer: evidence has a ranking. First comes the *written document* (likhita), then *witnesses* (sākṣin), then actual *possession* (bhukti, who has held the land and for how long), and last *inference* (yukti), reasoning from circumstances. The divine ordeals, where the gods were asked to reveal the truth, were pushed down to a final resort.

The odd thing is that this text does not survive as a whole. Medieval commentators quoted it constantly, and from those quotations scholars (Julius Jolly in 1889, and later others) pieced it back together. A teaching can be so useful that the quotations alone keep it alive.

The text also separates the procedure for compensation (civil) from the procedure for punishment (criminal), and lays down rules for appeal, on the principle that even a court can be wrong.

📖 *Day 67*

*Artha-śāstra*
_A hard-headed science of how a state is run._

*Artha* does not mean money alone. It means the material means of life and the machinery that protects them: farms, taxes, trade, forts, armies, spies, courts. The science of all this is the *Arthaśāstra*.

The central text is Kauṭilya's, written in the Mauryan age (about the fourth century BCE), some seventeen centuries before Machiavelli's The Prince. For ages it was known only through quotations, until a full manuscript was identified in a Mysore library in 1905.

In this section you will meet:

1️⃣ *Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra*: the original and widest manual.
2️⃣ *Kāmandakīya Nītisāra*: its elegant verse summary.
3️⃣ *Śukra-nīti*: a practical encyclopedia, from town-planning to testing physicians.
4️⃣ *Manu's Rāja-dharma*: polity treated as a branch of dharma.

These books do not only teach how power is won. They also ask how it is kept, and how a ruler serves the people he governs.

📖 *Day 68*

*Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra*
_A chief minister's handbook for running a state._

In 1905, R. Shamasastry found a palm-leaf manuscript in a Mysore library, and a lost book returned to the world. It was Kauṭilya's *Arthaśāstra*.

Kauṭilya, also called Cāṇakya, was chief minister to Candragupta Maurya. In fifteen books he covers the training of a prince, the appointment of officers, law, taxes, war and the running of an intelligence network. He begins by saying:

*Kāmandakīya Nītisāra*
_The Arthaśāstra in polished verse, carried as far as Java and Bali._

Kauṭilya's work is long and dense with prose. By the seventh or eighth century a courtier could hardly consult it every day. So Kāmandaka condensed its core into twenty cantos of graceful Sanskrit verse. Verse can be memorised, and a memorised line comes to mind exactly when it is needed.

Kāmandaka is more openly moral than Kauṭilya. He insists that politics stay within dharma. Manuscripts of the work survive across India and also in Java and Bali.

📖 *Day 70*

*Śukra-nīti*
_Towns, coins, physicians and horses: the everyday business of rule._

Śukrācārya is the preceptor of the asuras, the figure tradition credits with the life-restoring Mṛtasañjīvanī vidyā. The *Śukra-nīti* that bears his name is in fact much later. Where Kauṭilya is sharp on statecraft and Kāmandaka on foreign policy, the Śukra-nīti is the book of day-to-day administration. Its opening chapter also speaks plainly about bribery, favouritism and abuse of office, and how to remedy them.

*मनुस्मृती: राजधर्म*

मनुस्मृतीतले सातवे आणि आठवे अध्याय म्हणजे ग्रंथाच्या आत लपलेलं एक स्वतंत्र अर्थशास्त्र. सातव्यात राजाची उत्पत्ती, त्याचं कर्तव्य, राज्याची सात अंगं, मंत्र्यांचे गुण आणि युद्धनीती येते. आठवा हा मनुस्मृतीतला सर्वात लांब अध्याय आहे, आणि त्यात अठरा व्यवहारपदं (खटल्यांचे अठरा प्रकार) मांडलेले आहेत, कर्जापासून बदनामीपर्यंत.

मनूचा मूळ विचार असा: राजा आहे कारण कोणीतरी रक्षण करायला हवं. त्याचा *दण्ड* प्रजेवर हुकमत गाजवण्यासाठी नाही, तर दुर्बलांचं रक्षण करण्यासाठी आहे.

संघर्ष सोडवण्याचा क्रम असा:

1️⃣ *साम*: समजूत
2️⃣ *दान*: देणं
3️⃣ *भेद*: फूट पाडणं
4️⃣ *दण्ड*: बळ

पहिला उपाय फसला तरच पुढचा.

करांबद्दलची एक उपमा लोकप्रिय आहे: गाईचं दूध काढताना तिला चारा द्यावा लागतो. राजानं उत्पन्नाचा अधिशेष घ्यावा, मूळ भांडवल नव्हे. (काही नियम त्या काळाचे प्रतिबिंब आहेत, आणि आज त्यावर चर्चा होते.)

📖 *Day 71*

*Manusmṛti: Rāja-dharma*
_Politics as a branch of dharma._

Chapters seven and eight of the Manusmṛti form a small Arthaśāstra hidden inside a larger code. Chapter seven covers the origin of the king, his duty of protection, the seven limbs of the state, the qualities of ministers and the principles of war. Chapter eight is the longest in the book and sets out the eighteen titles of law, from debt to defamation.

Manu's basic idea: a king exists because someone must protect. His *daṇḍa* (coercive power) is meant to guard the weak, not to assert his will.

For settling conflict he gives a sequence:

1️⃣ *Sāma*: conciliation
2️⃣ *Dāna*: gifts
3️⃣ *Bheda*: sowing division
4️⃣ *Daṇḍa*: force

One moves to the next only when the earlier one has failed.

A well-known image on taxation says a cow must be fed before she can be milked. The king takes the surplus, not the substance. (Some rules here reflect their age and are debated today.)

📖 *Day 72*

*Kāma-śāstra*
_The science of cultivated living, with kāma as one of life's aims._

Indian tradition names four aims of human life: dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa. The third, *kāma*, means desire, pleasure and living with refinement. Instead of hiding it, the tradition made it a subject of study.

The central text here is Vātsyāyana's *Kāma-sūtra*, which is far from its modern reputation. Of its seven books only one deals with the sexual act. The rest speak of the cultivated city-dweller's home, friendships, arts, courtship and relationships.

In this section you will meet:

1️⃣ *Kāma-sūtra*: Vātsyāyana's original text.
2️⃣ *Jayamaṅgalā*: Yaśodhara's famous commentary, the lens through which most readers meet the text.

It is a precious source for the urban society, the arts and the education of women in classical India.

*कामसूत्र*
_वात्स्यायनाचा रसिक आणि सुसंस्कृत जगण्याचा ग्रंथ._

📖 *Day 73*

*Kāma-sūtra*
_Vātsyāyana's book on a refined and cultivated life._

Look at how the book itself defines kāma: the engagement of ear, skin, eye, tongue and nose, each with its proper object, guided by the mind in union with the self (1.2.11). That is not bodily pleasure alone. Listening to music, smelling flowers, a good meal, a beautiful sight, all of it is kāma.

Vātsyāyana Mallanāga (about the third century CE) wrote it as a condensation of larger older works. He names the earlier teachers himself: Bābhravya, Dattaka, Suvarṇanābha and others.

His ideal is the *nāgaraka*, the cultivated citizen. He should know the sixty-four arts, among them music, painting, cookery, poetry and conversation. His home is well kept, his friends are good, and his relationships are conducted with courtesy.

When dharma, artha and kāma conflict, Vātsyāyana says dharma outranks artha, and artha outranks kāma. (The author of the Kāma-sūtra is generally thought to be a different person from the Vātsyāyana of the Nyāya commentary.)

*जयमंगला*
_यशोधराची टीका, ज्यातून आपण कामसूत्र वाचतो._

📖 *Day 74*

*Jayamaṅgalā*
_Yaśodhara's commentary, the lens through which we read the Kāma-sūtra._

The word *sūtra* means "thread". Sūtras were written in very few words so they could be memorised, but without a teacher they are often hard to follow. Vātsyāyana's Kāma-sūtra is just as compressed and allusive.

Around the thirteenth century Yaśodhara Indrapāda wrote the *Jayamaṅgalā*, and the text became readable. He unpacked the sūtras into flowing prose, gave identities to the earlier teachers Vātsyāyana only names, and settled many ambiguous passages.

Most printed editions and translations today draw their readings from this commentary. Even the Kāma-sūtra that reached European scholars in the nineteenth century came by this route.

So the Kāma-sūtra we hold in our hands comes to us through Yaśodhara's eyes. It is a lovely reminder that a commentator can matter almost as much as the author.

📖 *Day 75*

*Kāvya & Nāṭya*
_Epic, drama, lyric and tale: four halls of Sanskrit literature._

Sanskrit literature is not only Vedas and treatises. It also holds a dazzling world of poets, dramatists and storytellers, where language celebrates its own beauty.

This section has four halls:

1️⃣ *Mahākāvya*: the ornate court epics of Kālidāsa, Bhāravi, Māgha and Śrīharṣa.
2️⃣ *Nāṭya*: the plays of Bhāsa, Śūdraka and Kālidāsa, and Bharata's Nāṭya-śāstra.
